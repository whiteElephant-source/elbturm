History of the Elbtower

In the beginning of the 19th century, Hamburg’s shipping companies started to collaborate with South American merchants, leading to an explosion of trade and a densification construction in Hamburg’s ports. Its first artificial and modern docking port for steam boats was constructed in 1868. Continuously more and more parts were added to the conglomerate situated in the river Elbe. Within twenty years, the whole island was covered with port installations. In the 20th century some of the first heated and refrigerated warehouses were built. 

After most of it was destroyed during the second world war, Hamburg started to rebuild the area again. When in 1956 the freight tanker was invented, the city needed more space and began to relocate the industrial areas outside of the city where space was not a problem. In the last decades of the 20th century, the inner city ports and their warehouses lost significance compared to the more modernised freight stations in the south. 

In 1997, the senate of Hamburg decided to redevelop the whole former port area into “HafenCity”. Its development should make space for the increase of inhabitants and its profits should finance the expansion of the new Industrial Port Altenwerder.

The HafenCity project in Hamburg, one of the largest urban redevelopment efforts in Europe, began in April 1999 when an international competition for ideas was announced to transform and expand Hamburg’s „new downtown.“ This ambitious project aimed to increase the city‘s urban area by 40% over 25 years. Six months later, a Dutch-German team composed of HamburgPlan, Kees Christiaanse, and ASTOC wins the competition. Their proposal would guide the development of HafenCity, turning former port areas into “a vibrant urban district, blending residential, commercial, and cultural spaces”. In February 2000, the Hamburg Senate approved the HafenCity Masterplan, formalising the urban development concept, which was then opened to public discussion.

From the outset, HafenCity’s development is designed to meet the evolving needs of Hamburg’s growing population, particularly the rising number of one- and two-person households and older buyers who prefer quality over quantity in their housing choices. In 2004, a demographic forecast predicted that the city would gain an additional 80,000 inhabitants, or 60,000 households, by 2020 - a prediction that remained largely accurate over time. In response to these needs, the HafenCity development aims to create new residential spaces for up to 12,000 people and generate 40,000 new jobs, turning it into a major hub for living and working. 

By 2002, a development agency, the newly founded Gesellschaft für Hafen- und Stadtentwicklung GmbH (GHS), later renamed HafenCity Hamburg GmbH, took over the area and managed the implementation of its projects. This quasi-autonomous non-governmental organisation was created to ease the development process. It acquired most of the land in HafenCity, becoming a central player in the execution of the project’s goals.

By 2004, the first buildings in HafenCity were completed, marking the early stages of the transformation. With the demand for housing growing and HafenCity offering a modern, mixed-use district, the project gains momentum. As originally predicted in 2000, the implementation phase was expected to last about 25 years. One of the crown jewels of the redevelopment is planned to be the Elbtower, which would sit at the eastern edge of the district, towering over the rest of the city as a symbol of Hamburg‘s aspirations for modern urban development.

In 2017, HafenCity Hamburg GmbH published a competition for the design of the Elbtower, the final major construction of HafenCity. The competition follows a restricted procedure, and on March 15, 2017, the result was announced via an „ex-ante transparency notification“ in the EU Official Journal. The project has a total procurement value of €120 million, with estimated construction costs of around €700 million. The competition is won by British architect David Chipperfield in collaboration with Austrian real estate giant Signa Holding GmbH. The Elbtower is designed to stand 245 metres high, set to become an architectural landmark on the city skyline. (1)

On February 8, 2018, Hamburg’s mayor Olaf Scholz publicly presented the Elbtower as a „mega project“ that would further enhance the city’s global standing. Scholz expresses his desire for the citizens of Hamburg to one day recognize his role in the successful completion of the project. „Ich als Bürgermeister möchte, dass die Hamburger sagen, das hat Scholz gut gemacht“ (2) He clearly underlines the political and personal pride associated with such a towering structure. 
The Elbtower’s height and prominence would serve as a testament to Hamburg’s economic success and urban modernization in the 21st century.

However, even as the Elbtower gains attention, questions emerge. Two other bidders had submitted offers that exceeded Signa‘s by €12 million and €15 million, but their proposals were not selected. The decision to go with Signa’s bid was met with some scepticism, but political support for the tower remains strong. (3)

In March 2019, the Hamburg Parliament (Bürgerschaft) formally approved the sale of the Elbtower plot to Signa. A contract is established that includes safeguard provisions, such as a buyback right (Rückkaufsrecht), to protect the city from incurring costs in case the project is not completed. The contract ensures that if there were construction delays or if the tower became a speculative ruin, the city would have the right to repurchase the property, thereby avoiding financial losses. 

Construction work begins with test pile drilling for the foundation reaching a depth of 111.4 metres in August 2019, setting a record for the longest bored piles in Germany. In December 2020, Signa submitted the formal building application, and by January 2021, the city approves the development plan for the Elbtower. In September 2021, the structural contract for the tower was awarded to Adolf Lupp GmbH, and in October 2021, partial approval for the construction of the excavation pit and foundation components was granted. The project is now moving toward its target completion date of late 2025.

Despite the steady progress on construction, financial issues begin to arise. Signa does not yet reach the preletting rate needed to confidently start construction. In early 2022, Signa Holding makes a significant acquisition, purchasing the old headquarters of the Hamburg Commercial Bank (HCOB) on Gerhart-Hauptmann-Platz for 220 million euros, just as the Elbtower receives its final approvals. HCOB describes its former location as „technically outdated,“ citing sustainability concerns. This deal comes with modernization costs of at least 2,000 euros per square metre for the 30,000 square metres of the former bank headquarters, adding another 60 million euros to Signa‘s financial burdens. To finance this deal, HCOB lends Signa 166 million to purchase their own building. In exchange, HCOB agrees to lease 11,000 square metres of office space on the upper floors of the Elbtower, helping Signa meet its pre-letting requirements. The price per square metre for those floors is 38 euros per square metre, thirteen percent higher than the top rent in Hamburg.

By August 2022, Commerz Real AG, a subsidiary of Commerzbank and one of Europe’s largest real estate investors, acquired a 25% stake in the Elbtower project. However, the scope of the development had grown, and with it, the cost. In November 2022, according to Signa‘s CEO, Timo Herzberg, the tower’s foundation was completed three weeks ahead of schedule. However, by late 2023, problems are beginning to show. In October, construction comes to a halt due to unpaid bills, with the tower reaching only 100 metres of its intended 245-metre height. 

On November 8, 2023, René Benko stepped down as the head of Signa Holding amidst growing financial turmoil. On November 28, Signa Holding declared bankruptcy, citing liquidity issues that could not be resolved despite significant restructuring efforts. Insiders report that Signa‘s total debts had reached approximately €2.2 billion.

With Signa‘s bankruptcy, the future of the Elbtower becomes uncertain. Hamburg’s Urban Development Senator, Karen Pein, expresses a desire for investors to complete the project. If this fails, the city must invoke its buyback right, which expires on January 31, 2025. (4)
Under the terms of the contract, the city could repurchase the plot for the original sale price of €122 million (minus €5 million in deductions), and no added interest. Considering the current value of the partially built structure, estimated at a minimum of €300 million, the city would essentially receive the tower “for free” if it took over the project. (5)

In early 2024, the Zech Group, a major construction firm, real estate developer, and property manager, emerged as a potential new investor. Zech Group has the advantage of being able to complete the tower without needing to meet profit margins required by other investors. However, completing the tower in its original form poses several challenges. (6)
If the new investors wanted to alter the design or reduce the tower’s height, it would require new building permits, leading to delays and additional costs. David Chipperfield Architects hold legal rights to the tower’s design and the completion of the tower would have to happen under their supervision.

In March 2024, René Benko filed for individual enterprise insolvency in Austria, which allows him to negotiate debt repayment on more favourable terms. Unlike a standard insolvency case, this option provides flexibility, enabling Benko to repay a portion of his debt without being forced into liquidation. When enacted, after an initial repayment towards the bondholders, an individual enterprise has three years to deal with the insolvency case. After, if they were not able to collect all the necessary funds to repay their debt, the case is dropped and the debt is left unsettled. 

Normally, after an individual enterprise insolvency it is expected to file for a restructuring process. By putting the pressure on a small part of the company, the rest could recover and avoid collateral damage. Rene Benko did not file for a restructuring process. The lack of a formal restructuring plan left the future of the Elbtower project in limbo.

The Elbtower, once envisioned as the crowning achievement of HafenCity’s redevelopment, now stands partially built, symbolising both Hamburg’s modern ambitions and the risks of large-scale urban projects. 

The insolvency administrator is negotiating with potential investors. The offer period for the tower expired in October. A decision is expected to be made this autumn. By January 2025 it will then be determined whether the City of Hamburg will withdraw from its repurchase right and thus allow the tower to be completed by a new investor. The project’s outcome will depend on whether new investors step in and overcome the financial and logistical challenges, or if the city will take control and chart a new course for the tower. As of November 15th 2024, after our conversation with the project manager Daniel Wendler at Chipperfield, it much looks like the tower might be completed. 

Whether it will be completed as initially planned, redesigned, face further delays or fail again remains to be seen.
