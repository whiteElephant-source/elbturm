A ruined building and an unfinished one, negate their finiteness and invite for their perpetual continuation.

In the southern hemisphere, unfinished buildings are not generally considered unworthy. In Egypt and Peru alike – to name just two examples – fiscal policy taxes only buildings that are completed. As long as a building has no roof, no matter its number of floors, it is considered unfinished. To avoid taxation people simply leave the reinforcement bars sticking out the top of their houses. But the reason for buildings to remain without fixtures and finishings is not just a fiscal one. The unfinished buildings also encapsulate an idea and a possibility of growth. One imagines that when wealth increases, or when families grow, the house does so, too. Here protruding reinforcement bars provide for the case that the need to build strikes again. In Peru and Egypt, whilst the current generation might not be able to afford to live as comfortably as they would like, there is a cross-generational provision. Houses stay unfinished to allow for further growth in future generations. Big houses are a symbol of potential status.
